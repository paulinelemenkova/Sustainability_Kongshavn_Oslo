% Abstract (Do not insert blank lines, i.e. \\) 
\abstract{In many civil engineering problems, soil is stabilised by a combination of binders and water in parameterised proportions taken as factors. If the conditional ratio of stabilizing agents is known through modelling, then solidification of soil can be achieved by sampling the specimens at a finite number of trials and checking each test to see if the target stabilization degree is achieved. The success of stabilization is then evaluated using the seismic non-destructive tests with measured P-wave velocities passing through samples. Optimisation of process, laboratory testing and data modelling is therefore essential prior to construction works to reduce the costs of the industrial projects where large quantities of soil are to be stabilised. This paper reports the optimised workflow of soil stabilisation through evaluated effects from the two factors controlling the development of strength. In particular, (1) the ratio between water and binder, and (2) the proportions of different binders (cement/slag) were changed experimentally in a mixture of samples to evaluate the strength of soil with various amount of water and combination of binders. The experimental results show optimal combination of stabilising agents: 30\% cement (EN 197-1) and 70\% slag (EN 15167-1) with a binder content of $120 kg/m^3$ and a maximum water binder ratio (w/b) of 5. Such proportions of mixture demonstrated effective soil stabilization both on a pilot test scale and on full scale for industrial works. The correlation between compressive strength and relative deformation of specimens was investigated revealed that the strength is the highest values for w/b = 5 and the lowest for w/b = 7. In the case of high water content in soil and wet samples, the condition of a w/b $\le$ 5 will require a higher amount of binder.}

\section{Introduction}

Many civil engineering and construction projects in harbours face the challenges of recycling of dredged marine sediments \cite{LIM2020110869,Lindh202208,Wangetal2022}. To solve this problem, many approaches have been developed to fabricate safe and environmentally-friendly construction materials from dredged sediments. The examples of the reuse of recycled marine sediments as building materials include manufacture of bricks \cite{HUSSAIN2022100046}, construction of roads \cite{Wangetal2017} and pavements \cite{Yoobanpot}. These include solidification/stabilisation (s/s) of specimens to increase soil strength and workability \cite{ZHANG2020138551}, removal and immobilisation of toxic substances and contaminants through leaching of heavy metals and pollutants \cite{SLAMA2021127,Lemenkova202233,ZHANG2021128926} and reducing high costs of works related to management and reuse of dredged sediments \cite{WANG201869}. Solidification/stabilisation (s/s) has been proposed to increase bearing capacity of soil through improved strength and the results are encouraging \cite{Yongfeng,Lindh202225,Rashidi,Zhangetal2011}. A popular approach to s/s processing of soil is to make use of binders that serve as stabilising agents, and then apply geotechnical methods of evaluating soil strength and environmental properties before reuse \cite{Rios,ZHANG2022127996,Lindh202302}. 

Stabilisation of soil with binders requires considering soil properties which differs significantly according to the soil types and structure: fine-grained clays, middle-grained sands or coarse-grained gravel. In various types of soil, key physio-mechanical parameters differ significantly, such as mineral content, moisture, density, cohesion and plasticity, swelling potential, volumetric weight and porosity, etc \cite{Grubb,Wangetal2018}. Therefore, s/s treatment typically requires the selection of the optimised types of binders adjusted to soil types aimed at improving the characteristics of strength, compressibility, and bulking \cite{Grubbetal2010}. 

The literature abounds with diverse approaches of various stabilization techniques to improving the properties of soil which typically include major binders (cement, slag, fly ash or lime) and their blended mixtures. For example, the effects of fly ash content were investigated in stabilised gravel \cite{Nordmark}, clay \cite{Min} and soft soil \cite{Taj}. The use of cement, slag and nanosilica was successfully tested to increase the freezing-thawing durability in soft clays \cite{Eissa}. The benefits of ash-slag cementitious materials were reported for solidification of soft soils \cite{ShuJing}. The effects of improved lime-based binders were evaluated with tested different proportions of slag \cite{Muthukkumaran}, cement \cite{Lindh202226} and ash \cite{Lietal2019} in the admixture content for stabilisation of clayey soils. the effects of various types of cement and limestone \cite{Bazne} and cement/slag blends \cite{Lindh202222} were assessed to analyse the behavior of high-moisture content fine-grained soils after stabilisation. These and related approaches are based on proposing more efficient binder recipes to improve the structure and engineering properties of soil.   

New mainstream directions in civil and geotechnical engineering include the use of optimised blends of novel admixtures besides traditional binders used in engineering \cite{NAGY,Yi,Lindh202305,Ismail}. Such improvements aim at increasing the long-term effects from novel admixtures on soil strength and thus to ensure more effective soil stabilisation. With this regard, yet another factor in soil stabilisation is a period of treatment \cite{Romera}. As a general rule, the effects of s/s process increase with curing time which is reflected in the development of physico-mechanical properties of soil. For instance, existing studies reported difference in mineralogical evolution, compressive strength, porosity, and pH of s/s soil and cemented materials over curing time, according to the tests taken in key control days \cite{Chrysochoouetal2010,Lemenkova202131,Romera}. 

However, besides the selection of binder types, the effective soil stabilisation requires a dosage of suitable binders and the addition of water in balanced proportions. Hence, water-binder ratio is another important factor in s/s treatment techniques aimed at improving poor engineering properties of soil. 

Seismic sonic methods have been with great success for evaluation of strength properties of stabilised soil \cite{Lopes,Lindh202210,CRISTELO20151}. The goal of such methods is to evaluate the increase in cementation of soil through recorded P-wave's velocity which increases along with stiffness and strength of materials \cite{ZHANG2021143,Lemenkova202139,Abdi}. Hence, the acoustic acquisition and calculation of elastic wave velocities serves as an indicator of success in soil stabilisation. 

From a pure civil engineering perspective, it would seem more reasonable to model each soil sample as the superposition of factors that include variations in binder proportions and water content \cite{Bumanis,HAN2022e01660,HAY2023131529}. Shared across all specimens of the same soil probe, such experiments enable to evaluate the effects of water-binder dosage on stabilisation process with regard to the individual properties of soil: density, moisture, grain size, type, texture, content of clay/sand, amount of organic matter. This representation would allow us to account for the differences between the distribution of the mentioned above parameters in the same class of soil samples. Furthermore, it would let us discard the non-linearity part of the soil modelling process when evaluating the relationship between soil texture and types of binders, which is relevant for analysis of geotechnical properties of soil since it carries information about strength parameters and focus on soil hardening through the optimised workflow. 

In light of the above discussion, in this paper we study the benefits of exploiting the effects of optimised binder proportions and water content on strength and deformation of soil. The optimised workflow is proposed to achieve the most effective procedure and reduce high costs of soil processing in construction industry. The best combination of stabilizing agents for soil treatment ensures the control of the soil quality for geotechnical suitability of foundations before construction works in a harbour. To this end, we introduce a new method for improving the stabilization efficiency by setting up an updated sampling procedure which included 4 Batches of specimens. Thus, we use the activated carbon (powdered charcoal) for pre-treatment of soil materials one month before the stabilization, and apply the Belite Calcium Sulfoaluminate (BCSA) type cement which has environmentally quality compared to the Portland cement due to low $CO_2$ emissions. the soil pre-treated with these materials demonstrated better environmental characteristics.

\section{Materials and Methods}

%----------- subsection ----------->
\subsection{Soil homogenisation and selection of binders}

The selection of binders used for stabilization of soil was adjusted according to our previous studies and several similar projects in Sweden. In the mixing tests, a binder combination of 30\% CEM I (SS-EN 197-1) \cite{SIS2011} and 70\% slag (SS-EN 15167-1) \cite{SIS2006} was selected as a the most effective combination. Figure \ref{fig01} shows the strengths for different combinations of cement and slag and variations in strength, accordingly. The best effect on strength development was obtained at a slag admixture of 70\% of the total amount of binder. The tests were implemented using various concentrations of water in a mixture of sediments collected from the Port of Oslo with respect to the amount of binder and water/binder ratio (w/b). The selection of the most optimal w/b value was argued based on the previous research performed in Arendal 2 and Gothenburg (southern Sweden) (Lindh and Lemenkova, 2021).

During the testing procedure, the experiments were carried out with w/b of 5, 6 and 7 of the dredged materials, where the lowest level corresponds to the highest amount of binder in relation to the amount of water in the dredged materials, i.e., "high binder-low water" $H_BL_W$. Accordingly, the highest level of w/b indicates the highest percentage of water and the lowest amount of binder. The ratio of water/binder in fabricated specimens is reported in Table 3.

Because compressive strength and UCS behavior of stabilised soil specimens can change dramatically over time and under different combinations binders and water/binder proportions, more than one test should be used to provide information necessary for characterising the potential stiffness and strength behavior of soil. Therefore, we used samples of soil collected in several test tites (TS) as specimens stabilised using various w/b percentage to analysis soil performance. A total of 70 test samples were fabricated. For w/b 5 and 6, five specimens were fabricated for each sample test point. Normally, three specimens were used for strength testing and seismic measurements, one for permeability tests and one specimen was in reserve. These reserve specimens were also used in some cases, when some specimens were damaged during demoulding. For the two of the sample points (Sample No. $1\_2$ and Sample No. 3), samples were also produced with w/b 7 to check the robustness and reliability of the recipe.

Before mixing binders into the dredge masses, each dredge mass was homogenised for at least five minutes using mixer. After homogenisation, a certain amount of dredged material and binder was weighed. To calculate the amount of binder, the water content of the dredged material was calculated based on the water ratio and density of the dredged material, see Table \ref{tab01}.

The clayey soil masses and binders were mixed during 5 minutes to obtain a good homogeneity. As a form of manufacturing the test bodies, the piston sampling sleeves were used, according to Swedish practice, see Figure \ref{fig01} (b) showing a standard sampling sleeve. The sleeve has a diameter of approximately 50 mm and a height of 170 mm. The sleeve in the photo is fitted with a lower cover. During the fabrication of the samples, the stabilized dredge masses were placed in the sleeves in several layers. Between each new layer, the sleeves were tapped on a table several times to expel any air that may be trapped in the sleeve during filling. After the fabrication of the test samples, the sleeves were placed into a water bath, i.e. stored under water. 

After 14 days, the specimens were de-moulded. Upon demoulding, the samples were trimmed to a length of 100 mm. This means that the samples have a slenderness factor of 2, i.e., the samples have a height corresponding to twice the diameter. This is according to Swedish standard practice and means that ordinary geotechnical testing can be carried out without the conversion factors. 

%----------- subsection ----------->
\subsection{Seismic measurements}

Seismic measurements on the samples can be carried out using a special equipment where the natural frequency of the samples is measured. Based on this frequency, the compression wave velocity (VP) of the samples, also called P-wave velocity, can be calculated. The P-wave velocity is linked to the material's modulus of elasticity (\emph{E}). However, in this case, the P-wave is measured at different times to evaluate the development of the strength in specimen with time. The equipment for measuring P-wave velocity is and a process of seismic measurements which evaluates P-wave velocity of the specimen followed the existing scheme \cite{Lindh202224}. The measurement of the resonance frequency was performed at different time periods to evaluate the changes over time which indicate the gain in strength in soil specimens. 

%----------- subsection ----------->
\subsection{Compressive strength measurements}

After trimming of specimens and measuring their resonance frequency, the specimens were placed into the plastic bags. To ensure high humidity in the plastic bags, a piece of moistened paper was placed in each plastic bag. After curing, the samples were pressed to failure in a 100 kN pressure press of the brand MTS, see Figure \ref{fig02} showing the MTS 810 servohydraulic universal testing machine used for test compressing the specimens.

\begin{figure}[H]
\begin{adjustwidth}{-\extralength}{0cm}
\centering
	\hspace{100pt}\includegraphics[width=9.0 cm]{Fig_02.jpg}
\end{adjustwidth}
\caption{MTS 810 servohydraulic universal testing machine with maximum force capacity of 10 t in compression/tension used for test compressive strength of the specimens. Photo: Per Lindh. \label{fig02}}

The loading rate was 1\% of the height of the specimen per minute, i.e., 1.0 mm/min, which is in accordance with the standard SS-EN 16907-4 \cite{SIS2018}. However, it should be mentioned that the Swedish Geotechnical Institute (SGI) uses 1.2 mm/min as adjusted to the local needs. However, this small difference has no significance for the determination of the strength of the soil material. In connection with these tests, the additional experiments were also carried out using the calorimeters to assess different reactions from different w/b and then evaluating the P-wave velocities in the samples, see Appendix 3.

\section{Conclusions}
The results from the lab tests both at LTH and at SGI show that the dredged masses are well suited to stabilize/solidify with an inorganic binder consisting of 30\% cement type CEM I (according to CEN 197-1) and 70\% ground granulated blast-furnace slag (GGBFS) according to EN 15167-1. The requirement for the amount of binder should be at least 120 kg/m3 with a secondary condition that the weight ratio between the weight of water contained and the weight of binder added should be $\leq$ 5. It should be pointed out that in the case of high water ratios in the dredged masses, more weight of water, the secondary condition will be a w/b $\leq$ 5 result in a higher amount of binder.
